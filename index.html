Big feelings are weather.
Weather passes.

Penny's feelings show up as Drizzle, a little cloud that floats over her head. When plans change, a game is lost or the room gets too loud, Drizzle's weather turns. Your child helps Penny notice it in her body, name it, and ride it out, one hard moment at a time.

Six kinds of weather

Drizzle gives kids a picture for what's going on inside, and a word for it that works at home and at school too: "How's your weather?"

  • 🌤️ClearReady for anything.
  • ☁️CloudySomething's bugging me.
  • 🌧️RainySad or disappointed.
  • ⛈️StormyToo big to think. Body first.
  • 💨WindyWound up and zooming.
  • 🌫️FoggyToo much. I need quiet.

The story

The Best Day Ever
doesn't go to plan.

Penny has planned a perfect Saturday in Frelard (that's Fremont plus Ballard, in Seattle): pancakes, the market, a bike ride, a picnic at the beach and salmon at the Locks. Then the pancakes burn, the bridge goes up, a big kid cuts the line and the picnic gets rained out.

  • Ten chapters on real Seattle streets and shores: the Fremont Bridge, the Burke-Gilman Trail, Golden Gardens and the Ballard Locks.
  • A cast to love: Penny, who knows every salmon fact there is; her Swedish grandma Mormor; careful Theo; Pip the dog; and Knut, a small troll who changes the rules when he's losing.
  • Plan B is sometimes worse than Plan A. The game says so out loud, and shows that she can handle it anyway.

Notice it, name it

Where do you feel it?

Lots of kids find their own body signals hard to read. So every hard moment starts with the body: tap where Penny feels it (hot cheeks, tight fists, a knot in her tummy), then pick the word that fits.

  • Twenty feeling words, each with a face, from mad and sad to disappointed, embarrassed and overwhelmed.
  • A word that doesn't fit isn't wrong. Penny says "Hmm, I don't think I'm that," and it gently goes away.
  • Two separate questions: how big is the feeling, and how big is the problem? A small problem can still bring a big feeling, and every feeling size is right.

Penny's Calm Kit

A backpack of things that help.

Twelve calming tools, each one a little game: blow a pinwheel, fill a balloon belly, push the wall, do ten big jumps, pet Pip, or find five things you can see.

  • When Drizzle is stormy, the thinking tools are greyed out. Body tools work first.
  • The Calm Kit is one tap from the title screen and works on its own, quietly: no story, no questions, no praise. Ready for a real hard moment.
  • Your child can pin their favourites to the front, and each day there's an optional calm exercise to practise.

Try it, then try again

The itchy choice is always there.

Real kids sometimes push, grab or yell, so Penny can too. The tempting option wobbles on the screen. Pick it, and the story plays out the way it really would: the friend walks away, the game ends, the salmon swims past.

  • Then Drizzle rewinds: "That's how it can really go. Let's rewind and try another way." A do-over, not a telling-off.
  • Other kids don't always listen, even to a kind ask. The game practises what to do next.
  • Asking a grown-up, letting it go and saying it "strong and kind" are all good answers.

Weather Reports and Adventures

Fire drills, scratchy tags and piñatas.

After the story, short Weather Reports cover everyday hard moments: a substitute teacher, a fire drill, screen time ending, a scratchy shirt, a friend who says "you're weird". Longer Adventures each teach one skill.

  • Taking turns talking: a talk ball shows whose turn it is, and Drizzle holds Penny's idea so waiting doesn't mean forgetting.
  • Joining a game that's already going, disagreeing kindly, and asking for help when you're stuck.
  • Getting no candy from the piñata, again and again, and stopping in the middle of something you love.

For grown-ups

Frelard Feelings was made by a Seattle parent, with ADHD and autistic kids in mind, and for any kid who feels things big. Here's what you can expect.

🌈 Neurodiversity-affirming

No masking, no forced eye contact. Penny's salmon obsession is never the problem. Repair comes before punishment, and every quiet option is a good option.

🗣️ The family's words

A one-page guide to the game's words ("How's your weather?", "body first", "big feeling or big problem?") to use at home and share with teachers, OTs or therapists.

📈 Help that fades

Each kind of hard moment (waiting, losing, transitions, loud places and more) tracks how much help your child needs, and the prompts fade as they get better at it.

🗓️ A real-life Forecast

Plan for tricky days the way Penny does: what might be hard, and what to pack. The parent area has a Forecast card to talk through together before the real thing.

⏳ No pressure

No timers, no streaks, no "you missed a day". Set a play window if you like; Mormor gives a warning, and the story always stops at a natural break.

🔒 Private by design

No ads, accounts, analytics or tracking, and the app never goes online. Feeling words and check-ins stay on the device. Privacy policy

🛡️ Grown-ups only, behind a gate

The parent area sits behind a grown-ups check. There you can hide tools, see the weather calendar, unlock every chapter or start over.

Frelard Feelings is a practice tool, not therapy or a diagnosis. It works best alongside the people who already help your child.
